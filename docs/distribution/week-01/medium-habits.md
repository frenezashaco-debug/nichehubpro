# 5 Questions to Ask Before Starting Another Habit

You have decided to read more. The book is ready, the goal sounds sensible, and the first attempt goes well. Then a meeting moves, breakfast happens on the way out, and the plan no longer has a place.

Before deciding you need more motivation, examine the opportunity you expected to have. A habit plan belongs in an actual day, not a version of your day with no interruptions.

These five questions turn a broad intention into a small planning experiment. They do not promise automatic behavior on a deadline.

## 1. What action would count?

"Read more" describes a direction. "Open the book and read one page after breakfast" describes an action you can recognize.

One page is an example, not an evidence-based dose. Choose a starting point that is useful to you. If the small version has no value beyond preserving a streak, rethink the goal itself.

Write the action in a sentence without adding a second ambition. You can decide about reading a whole chapter after you have tried the first plan.

## 2. Does the reminder actually happen?

A reminder at a fixed time may not fit a changing schedule. An existing activity can be another option, but only if it reliably happens in a suitable place.

Imagine a reader who usually eats breakfast at home but travels on Wednesdays. Attaching reading to breakfast may fit some days and fail on others. That is information about the cue, not evidence of weak commitment.

This is an illustrative scenario rather than a personal success story. Its purpose is to show the choice that needs to be made: use another opportunity on travel days, or leave those days outside the plan.

## 3. What preparation gets in the way?

Try walking through the first step before promising yourself a long routine. Is the book in another room? Does the activity require an app, a login, or equipment you have not prepared?

Remove one avoidable obstacle. Leave the book where you intend to read, or choose a place where the task is possible without disturbing someone else.

Preparation should serve the action. Buying a new notebook or rebuilding a tracking system can become a separate project that delays the thing you wanted to do.

## 4. What will happen when the opportunity disappears?

Choose a response in advance: move the attempt, shorten it, or skip it and resume at the next suitable opportunity.

There is no need to invent a punishment or double tomorrow's workload. If your circumstances have changed for a longer period, redesign the plan rather than accumulating missed tasks.

The [NIDDK's guide to changing habits](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diet-nutrition/changing-habits-better-health) discusses planning, tracking progress, and dealing with setbacks in health behavior change. It does not supply a universal deadline for every habit.

## 5. What would make you change the plan?

At the end of several opportunities, look at what happened:

- The cue was absent: choose a different opportunity.
- The materials were unavailable: reduce preparation.
- The action felt burdensome: reconsider its size or usefulness.
- The activity was useful: keep the current version before expanding it.

This review is a decision aid, not a validated assessment. You can write a few notes instead of recording a streak.
