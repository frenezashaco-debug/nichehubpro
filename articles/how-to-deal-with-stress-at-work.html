How to Deal with Stress at Work: 5 Steps for a Support Conversation

📅 2026-06-14 ⏳ 8 min read ✍️ NicheHubPro Editorial Team ✓ Sources listed

How to deal with stress at work can start with a concrete description of the pressure. Naming conflicting deadlines or missing support gives a conversation something practical to address.

A short pause may help you decide what to say, but it cannot remove understaffing, harassment or an unsafe task. Consider who can change the conditions as well as what you can do yourself.

This guide offers five steps for preparing a support conversation. It does not promise calm within minutes or require you to handle a difficult workplace alone.

How to deal with stress at work involves identifying the demand, its impact and the support or adjustment needed. Use the five conversation steps below to prepare a practical request, while seeking appropriate health or workplace assistance when necessary.

What is work-related stress?

Work-related stress concerns pressure connected with the job and how it affects you. It does not prove a personal weakness or identify a medical condition, so describe the work situation and its impact before choosing a response.

  • Name a specific demand, resource gap or difficult interaction.
  • Separate what happened from assumptions about other people's motives.
  • Identify who has the authority or responsibility to help.

What are the signs that you need support?

Stress can involve changes in concentration, sleep, mood or behaviour, but these are not specific enough for self-diagnosis. The NHS stress guide advises help when coping is difficult or self-help is not helping; discuss persistent difficulties with a professional.

  • Notice tasks or relationships affected by the pressure.
  • Do not automatically attribute new or concerning physical symptoms to stress.
  • Address immediate safety concerns through the appropriate local or workplace route.

Why might personal coping techniques leave the problem unresolved?

The WHO guidance on mental health at work identifies risks such as excessive demands, limited control and inadequate support. It recommends organisational action on working conditions, so include a practical workplace change in your plan where one is needed.

  • A deadline conflict may require a priority decision from someone else.
  • A missing resource may require staffing, equipment or training.
  • Harassment or unsafe conditions need appropriate reporting and support, not a requirement to become calmer.

How can five steps prepare a useful support conversation?

Choose a suitable person and a route that feels safe in your circumstances. Prepare one clear request rather than trying to justify your worth; these steps are an editorial aid, not a guarantee that the workplace will respond well.

  • 1. Describe the demand. Use a concrete example, such as two tasks due at the same time. Avoid including confidential information in an unapproved channel.
  • 2. Explain the practical impact. State what is becoming difficult, such as meeting both deadlines safely. Share personal health details only when you choose and through a suitable route.
  • 3. Request a specific adjustment. Ask which task takes priority, what can move or which resource is available. Leave room for a different workable option.
  • 4. Agree the next action. Clarify who will do what and how decisions will be recorded. If no agreement is possible, ask about an appropriate alternative support route.
  • 5. Set a review point. Choose when or under what change you will revisit the plan. Seek help sooner if the situation worsens or creates an immediate risk.

How can you manage the plan during a normal workday?

The UK HSE Management Standards distinguish six areas of work design: demands, control, support, relationships, role and change. Use them as prompts for identifying an unresolved issue; this guide's five conversation steps are not the HSE assessment process.

  • Keep a brief record of an agreed action and its owner.
  • Take an available break or pause if useful, without expecting it to fix the underlying condition.
  • Revisit the issue through a suitable workplace or health-support route when the plan is insufficient.

Conversation starter: Two tasks currently share the same deadline. I cannot complete both to the required standard in the available time. Which is the priority, and what should change for the other? Adapt the wording to your role, responsibilities and workplace procedures.

For a related difficulty, explore clarifying demands under pressure, responding to repeated work-related reviewing or finding support for ongoing anxiety.

An Illustrative Scenario

Fictional example: A coordinator receives two urgent assignments from different teams. They list the deadlines and ask the relevant manager to confirm the order of work. Try preparing one factual example before a support conversation rather than presenting every difficulty at once.

The example does not show that a manager agreed, stress decreased or a health problem resolved. It illustrates a request that makes a conflict visible; if the response is unhelpful, consider another suitable source of workplace or professional support.

Choose One Small Next Step

A clear request can make a work problem easier to discuss. It does not make you responsible for every part of its solution.

Support may involve a priority decision, a resource change or professional care. Choose the route that fits the actual problem.

Write down one demand and one question today. Use them to prepare a conversation with someone able to help.

Sources and Scope

WHO and NHS guidance provide health context; HSE describes a UK organisational framework. Our five conversation steps are a separate editorial planning aid, not a clinical treatment or a substitute for workplace procedures.
